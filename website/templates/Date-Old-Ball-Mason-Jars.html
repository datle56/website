Unveiling the Enchanting World of Dating Mason Jars: A Journey Through Time

Dive into the captivating history of Mason jars, the ubiquitous glass containers that have adorned our kitchens and graced our lives for centuries. From the intricate logos that adorn their surface to the subtle clues hidden in their colors and shapes, each Mason jar holds a wealth of knowledge just waiting to be uncovered. Part 1: The Evolution of the Mason Jar Logo

The iconic Mason jar has undergone a series of logo changes throughout its long history, each reflecting the evolving tastes and marketing strategies of the Ball Brothers Glass Manufacturing Company (BBGMC).

  • Early Buffalo Jars (1885-1886): The earliest Mason jars featured a simple logo with the image of a buffalo. These jars are rare and highly sought-after by collectors today.

  • Block Letters (1892-1896): The logo was simplified to bold, block letters without any cursive or styling.

  • First Script Logo (1895-1896): A cursive "Ball" lettering was introduced, with an underscore beneath it.

  • 3-L Logo (1900-1910): A decorative loop was added to the end of the logo, resembling a third "L".

  • Dropped A (1910-1923): The "A" in "Ball" began with a disconnected ascender, giving it a more whimsical look.

  • No Underscore (1923-1933): The underscore below the cursive "Ball" was dropped, creating a more streamlined logo.

  • Looped B (1933-1962): A loop was added to the center of the "B", adding an element of playfulness.

  • B Connected to Underscore (1960-Present): The lower part of the "B" now touches the line below, creating a more unified and modern logo.

Part 2: Additional Identifying Features

Beyond the logo, Mason jars often feature other identifying characteristics that help collectors date and authenticate them:

  • Christmas Lettering (1890): Some early jars were adorned with elegant calligraphy lettering that read "Masons Patent."

  • Identifying Words: Throughout the years, various words have been printed on Mason jars, including:

    • "Improved" (1890s, 1900-1933)
    • "Special" (1910-1913)
    • "Perfect" (1913-1922)
    • "Sanitary" (1913-1915)
    • "Ideal" (1915-1962)
    • "Square" (1925)
    • "Eclipse" (1926-1952)

  • Off-Size Jars: Some Mason jars were produced in unique sizes, such as 40-ounce coffee jars (1920s-1930s) and 42-ounce bootlegger jars.

  • Color: The color of Mason jars has varied over time, including blue (late 1890s-early 1900s), brown amber (1950s), and clear glass (post-1940s).

  • Mistakes: Occasionally, misspellings or other errors were printed on Mason jars, adding to their collectible value.

  • Upside-Down Logos (1900-1910): Some jars were produced with their logos facing upside down, as they were intended to be used as coffee dispensers.

Part 3: Marks to Ignore

While these identifying features can be helpful, there are some markings that collectors should ignore when dating Mason jars:

  • "1858" Patent Year: This refers to John Mason's patent date and does not necessarily indicate the actual year of production.

  • "1908" Patent Date: Another patent date not directly related to the production year.

  • Mold Number: This number indicates the position of the mold on the manufacturing machine and has no bearing on the date of production.


Conclusion:

The intricacies of Mason jar dating provide a fascinating glimpse into the history of glass manufacturing and American culture. From the early days of block letters to the distinctive cursive logos, each jar tells a unique story of craftsmanship and innovation. By understanding the various logos, identifying features, and mistakes, collectors can accurately date their jars and appreciate their rich heritage.

Whether you seek rare Buffalo jars or everyday "Ideal" jars, dating Mason jars adds a layer of charm and historical significance to these timeless vessels. They serve as tangible reminders of our past and continue to grace homes, kitchens, and gatherings, connecting us with a bygone era of ingenuity and domesticity.
