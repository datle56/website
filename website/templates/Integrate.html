Integration Basics

Integration, an indispensable mathematical operation, stands as the polar opposite of differentiation. Its primary purpose is to determine the area under the curve of a function. This operation is symbolized by the integral sign, ∫, followed by the integrand f(x) and the variable of integration x, written as ∫f(x)dx.

A fundamental theorem in integration, known as the power rule, provides a straightforward formula for integrating polynomial functions. It states that the integral of x^n with respect to x is equal to x^(n+1)/(n+1) + C, where C represents the constant of integration. This rule serves as the building block for integrating more complex functions.

Common Functions and Their Antiderivatives

To facilitate the integration process, it is essential to familiarize oneself with the antiderivatives of commonly encountered functions. These include:

  • Exponential: ∫e^x dx = e^x + C
  • Trigonometric:
    • ∫sin x dx = -cos x + C
    • ∫cos x dx = sin x + C
  • Inverse Trigonometric:
    • ∫sin^-1 x dx = sin^-1 x + C
    • ∫cos^-1 x dx = cos^-1 x + C
    • ∫tan^-1 x dx = tan^-1 x + C
  • Reciprocal: ∫1/x dx = ln |x| + C

Definite Integrals

In many real-world applications, it becomes necessary to calculate the area under a curve over a specific interval. This is where definite integrals come into play. The definite integral of a function f(x) over an interval [a, b] is written as ∫[a, b]f(x) dx.

The value of a definite integral is determined by evaluating the antiderivative of the function at the boundaries, b and a, and subtracting the two values. This quantity represents the net area under the curve within the specified interval.

Techniques for Integration

Integrating functions can sometimes prove challenging, but various techniques exist to simplify the process:

  • U-Substitution: This technique involves replacing a portion of the integrand, typically a complex expression, with a new variable u. This substitution simplifies the integration and allows for the introduction of a new antiderivative.
  • Integration by Parts: This technique is particularly effective for integrating products of functions. It involves breaking down the integrand into a simpler product minus the integral of the antiderivative of one function times the derivative of the other. This process is repeated until the integral can be easily evaluated.

Properties of Integrals

Integrals possess certain inherent properties that further enhance their utility:

  • Linearity: Integrals distribute over sums and multiples of functions, meaning that the integral of a sum is equal to the sum of the integrals, and the integral of a multiple is equal to the multiple of the integral.
  • Reversing the Order of Integration: For integrals involving even functions, the integral over [-a, a] is equal to twice the integral over [0, a]. Conversely, for odd functions, the integral over [-a, a] is zero.
