Unveil the Secrets of Latin Pronunciation: A Beginner's Guide

Embark on a linguistic adventure as we delve into the enchanting world of Latin pronunciation. Let us unlock the secrets of this ancient language, transforming the written word into the spoken eloquence that graced the lips of Roman orators and poets. Prepare to conquer the mysteries of consonants, vowels, and pronunciation rules, venturing into the specificities of Church Latin. Whether you're a curious language enthusiast or an aspiring Latin scholar, this beginner-friendly guide will equip you with the essential tools to navigate the complexities of this fascinating language.


Consonants

Pronunciation Details

"v" as "w"

  • In Classical Latin, "v" is always pronounced as a labiodental fricative represented by the English "w" sound.
  • Example: "via" (road) is pronounced as "wi-a."

"i" or "j" is always "y"

  • Both "i" and "j" are pronounced as the English "y" sound.
  • Example: "Iulius" (Julius) is pronounced as "Yoo-lee-us."

"c" is always hard

  • The letter "c" always represents a hard "k" sound, similar to the "c" in "cat."
  • Example: "cum" (with) is pronounced as "koom."

"g" is always hard

  • The letter "g" always represents a hard "g" sound, similar to the "g" in "go."
  • Example: "ago" (I do) is pronounced as "ah-go."

"s" is always "s"

  • In Classical Latin, "s" always represents the voiceless alveolar fricative, the "s" sound as in "sip."
  • Example: "spuma" (foam) is pronounced as "spoo-ma."

"r" is always rolled

  • The letter "r" is always pronounced with a trill, similar to the "r" in Spanish.
  • Example: "rosa" (rose) is pronounced with a rolled "r."

"h" is always pronounced

  • In Classical Latin, "h" is always aspirated and pronounced like the "h" in "hat."
  • Example: "hora" (hour) is pronounced as "hoh-ra."

Vowels

Pronunciation Details

Short vowels

  • There are five short vowels in Classical Latin: "a," "e," "i," "o," and "u."
  • They are pronounced similarly to their counterparts in English words like "alike," "let," "lick," "off," and "foot."
  • Example: "latus" (wide) is pronounced as "lah-toos."

Long vowels

  • Long vowels are held longer than short vowels and carry more stress.
  • They are represented by various diacritical marks (e.g., "ā," "ē," "ī," "ō," "ū").
  • Example: "pater" (father) is pronounced as "pah-tehr."

Diphthongs

  • Diphthongs are combinations of two vowels pronounced as a single syllable.
  • Common diphthongs in Classical Latin include "ae" (pronounced like "eye"), "au" (pronounced like "ow"), "ei" (pronounced like "ray"), and "oi" (pronounced like "oil").
  • Example: "caelum" (sky) is pronounced with the diphthong "ae."

Pronunciation Rules

Syllable Stress

  • In two-syllable words, stress is typically placed on the first syllable.

  • Example: "Caesar" is pronounced as "KAI-sar."

  • If the second-to-last syllable is heavy (i.e., contains a long vowel or diphthong), stress is placed on the second-to-last syllable.

  • Example: "aedes" (temple) is pronounced as "ah-EH-des."

  • If the second-to-last syllable is light (i.e., contains a short vowel), stress is placed on the syllable before it.

  • Example: "aper" (boar) is pronounced as "AH-pehr."

Church Latin Specifics

Consonant Modifications

  • Before the vowels "ae," "e," "oe," and "i," certain consonants are softened:
    • "c" becomes "ch" (like in "chain")
    • "g" becomes "j" (like in "gem")
    • "t" becomes "th" (like in "sheep")
  • Example: "caelum" (heaven) ispronounced as "cheh-lum."

Pronunciation Variations

  • The letter "v" is pronounced as the English "v" sound.
  • The combination "gn" is pronounced as "ni" (like in "onion").
  • The combination "ti" is pronounced as "tsy" (like in "patsy").

Other Notes

  • Double vowels are usually separated in pronunciation, but some exceptions occur.
  • Pronunciation exceptions to the general rules are relatively uncommon and are generally not covered for beginners.
