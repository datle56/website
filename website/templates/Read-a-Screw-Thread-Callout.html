Identifying American Screws Using Callouts

Step 1: Locate the Callout Number

The callout number is typically found on the packaging or shelf tag and is composed of a series of numbers and letters.

Step 2: Decipher the Diameter

The first number in the callout represents the screw's diameter, which is expressed in inches or as a number between 0 and 10. For example, #6 indicates a diameter of 0.138 inches, while #8 represents a diameter of 0.164 inches.

Step 3: Determine the Threads Per Inch (TPI)

The second number in the callout signifies the number of threads per inch (TPI). A higher TPI indicates a finer screw with more threads, while a lower TPI indicates a coarser screw with fewer threads. For instance, a screw with a callout of #6-32 has 32 threads per inch, while a #8-24 screw has 24 threads per inch.

Step 4: Identify the Thread Standard

The abbreviation following the TPI number represents the thread standard, which defines the shape and spacing of the threads. UNC (Unified Coarse) is the most common thread standard for general-purpose screws. Other common standards include UNF (Unified Fine), NF (National Coarse), and NF (National Fine).

Step 5: Determine the Tolerance Class (Optional)

If the callout includes a number from 1 to 3 after the thread standard, it represents the tolerance class. This indicates how tightly the screw fits into nuts or holes. Class 1 screws are the most precise, followed by Class 2 and Class 3.

Step 6: Identify Left-Handed Screws (Optional)

The presence of "LH" in the callout indicates that the screw is left-handed. This means that the screw loosens when turned clockwise, as opposed to the standard right-hand screws that tighten when turned clockwise.

Step 7: Determine the Screw Length

The final number in the callout represents the screw's length in inches. For example, a callout of #8-32 x 1.5 indicates a screw with a diameter of 0.164 inches, 32 threads per inch, and a length of 1.5 inches.

Identifying Metric Screws

Step 1: Locate the Callout Number

The callout number for metric screws starts with the letter "M," followed by a series of numbers.

Step 2: Determine the Diameter

The number immediately after the "M" in the callout indicates the screw's diameter in millimeters. For example, M4 represents a diameter of 4 millimeters, while M6 signifies a diameter of 6 millimeters.

Step 3: Identify the Pitch

The next number in the callout is the screw's pitch, which is the distance between threads in millimeters. A smaller pitch indicates a finer screw with more threads per centimeter, while a larger pitch indicates a coarser screw with fewer threads per centimeter. For instance, an M4 x 0.7 screw has a pitch of 0.7 millimeters, while an M6 x 1.0 screw has a pitch of 1.0 millimeter.

Step 4: Determine the Screw Length

The final number in the callout represents the screw's length in millimeters. For example, an M6 x 1.0 x 20 screw has a diameter of 6 millimeters, a pitch of 1.0 millimeter, and a length of 20 millimeters.


Conclusion:

Understanding the callout system is crucial for identifying both American and metric screws. By following the steps outlined above, you can decode the callout numbers and choose the correct screws for your projects. Whether you're a DIY enthusiast or a professional contractor, this knowledge will empower you to navigate hardware stores with confidence and ensure precise and reliable fastening. Remember to pay attention to the callout details, including diameter, threads per inch, standard, tolerance class, direction, and length, to ensure a successful assembly.
